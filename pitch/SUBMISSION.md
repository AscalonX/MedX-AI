Problem: when patient and clinic do not share a language, communication errors become safety events. In six US hospitals, adverse events in limited-English patients caused physical harm in 49.1% of cases vs 29.5% for English speakers (Divi 2007), and 13-59% of admission medication lists contain unintended errors.

MedX follows one patient journey, five roles:
- Patient (Thai, Italian or English) answers a guided intake by voice or text and confirms the transcript before sending; the phone later shows the pickup counter, never drug names.
- Nurse: a consult assistant listens during history-taking, links every summary field to the patient's own sentence, and suggests "ask next" and "rule out" questions. Vitals give a NEWS2-based triage suggestion the nurse confirms.

## Long description

**Problem.** When patient and clinic do not share a language, communication errors become safety events: in six US hospitals, adverse events in patients with limited English involved physical harm in 49.1% of cases, against 29.5% for English speakers (Divi et al. 2007). Medicine lists are fragile too: across six pharmacist-checked studies, 13% to 59% of patients had an unintended error in their admission medicine list. And doctors spend 49.2% of the office day on EHR and desk work, against 27.0% with patients (Sinsky et al. 2016).

**One journey, one record.** The *patient* tells Fin what brings them in, by voice or text, in Thai, Italian or English (English is the default), and checks every word before sending. The *nurse* sees a summary where every field links to the patient's own sentence, a consult assistant that listens during history-taking (after a recorded consent step) and pops up "ask next" and "rule out" questions, and a NEWS2-based triage suggestion the nurse confirms. The *doctor* gets doctor-only considerations from a fixed curated rule list (labelled "not a diagnosis", with guideline links), an evidence-linked draft note to sign, and orders with deterministic checks (allergy, interaction, duplicate, pregnancy, dose range); a high alert needs a written reason and lists options to review from a sourced alternatives table. **Pharmacist 1** verifies, or returns the order with a report and options for prescriber review; the system never writes an option into the order. **Pharmacist 2**, a different person, completes a six-item final check and dispenses; the server refuses the pharmacist who verified. The patient's phone then shows the pickup counter and code, never drug names. The clinic manager sees the queue, escalations, abstentions and the pharmacy pipeline, and every case has a trace of each rule check, model call and human decision.

**Try it.** Six synthetic roles on the landing page (Patient, Nurse, Doctor, Pharmacist 1, Pharmacist 2, Clinic operations). Each card signs you in as that person; staff cards open a fresh synthetic case at their step.

**Gemma and DeepSeek via AMD.** The live demo runs on the hackathon's AMD Token Factory, behind one OpenAI-compatible client with JSON-schema output, per-call timeouts inside a 52 s turn deadline, capped retries on HTTP 429 and one repair retry. Gemma-4-31B-IT runs the add-only red-flag safety screen and the next question; DeepSeek-V4-Flash runs the long structured extraction (fields, translation and the medicine list). We split them because, on the shared endpoint during the event, Gemma-4-31B-IT took 72 s and 300 s for one ~880-output-token extraction under strict JSON schema, beyond the 60 s budget of a serverless route, while short Gemma calls answer in a few seconds; DeepSeek-V4-Flash on the same Token Factory returned schema-valid extraction in 17 to 48 s. Every model call records its model id in the audit trail and the Case trace, and the model label on the site is read from `/api/health`. Safety checks are plain code. Call latency, successful calls only: Gemma screen p50 2.5 s / p95 10.6 s, DeepSeek extraction p50 25.9 s / p95 35.5 s, Gemma next question p50 4.1 s / p95 6.4 s. A whole patient turn took p50 38.4 s / p95 42.2 s, far above our 6 s / 12 s targets. The narrated walkthrough video was recorded on the deterministic mock model for reproducibility; the live site runs on AMD.

**Safety.** 18 deterministic red-flag rules run before any model and no model can clear them; a hit stops the intake with zero model calls. Fields without a verified patient quote show "Needs check", never as fact. Provider failure, schema failure or low confidence means abstain and "needs human review". The AI never diagnoses, prescribes or decides urgency; nothing is final until a clinician approves, and every action goes to an append-only audit log. Raw patient text is redacted before storage or any model call. The public URL has security headers, a rate limit and role checks on clinical writes.

**Evidence (synthetic, small n).** 52 synthetic evaluation cases (40 intake in three languages + 12 medication) and an 18-case blind red-flag holdout written without seeing the rules. Clinical rule checks: 175 red-flag, 253 medication-rule and 87 consult-rule reference tests, all passing. 2,451 automated tests passing (vitest, 53 files). End-to-end journey script against a real server: 113 of 113 steps passed in its last recorded run. On the blind holdout, keyword rules alone caught 7 of 10 emergencies with 3 of 8 false alarms, which is why the add-only model screen exists. With the Gemma screen on the AMD Token Factory: 8 of 10 (target 9 of 10, not met), false alarms unchanged at 3 of 8; the screen alone caught HO-04 (suicide risk), which the keyword rules missed. Test-split run on AMD (30 intake cases, 11 escalated by red-flag rules and excluded from field metrics; thresholds pre-declared): asserted field precision 88.1% (target ≥ 90%, not met); capture recall 26.2% (target ≥ 70%, not met); hallucination rate 3.4% (target ≤ 5%, met); unsupported claims with the verifier 11.9% against 12.5% without (target ≤ 5%, not met); in-sample red-flag case recall 100% (8 of 8, met) with 13.6% false alarms (target ≤ 10%, not met); turn latency not met (above). Capture recall is depressed by provider failures: the whole team shares one key's concurrency and spend limits, and only 34 of 111 calls succeeded (47 rejected with HTTP 429, 30 timed out). By design a failed call abstains to "needs human review"; it never guesses.

**Business case.** Buyers: private hospitals and clinics with international patients, and clinics in tourist areas (Thailand had 32.97 million foreign tourist arrivals in 2025). Pains: interpreter cost and throughput (CEO, CFO), documentation time (head nurse), AI they can verify (doctors), medication errors (quality team). Next step: a measured pilot at one clinic; we claim no savings figure.

**Limitations and safety.** Research prototype that supports clinicians; the AI never diagnoses, prescribes or decides urgency. Synthetic data only; not clinically validated, not a medical device. On the AMD Token Factory several pre-declared targets are not met: a patient turn takes p50 38.4 s, field precision is 88.1% (target 90%), unsupported claims 11.9% (target 5%), and capture recall 26.2% because most calls on one shared team key were rate-limited or timed out (each failure abstains to human review). The walkthrough video was recorded on the mock model. Red-flag rules run before any model and cannot be cleared by it, but miss paraphrases (7 of 10 emergencies on an 18-case synthetic holdout; 8 of 10 with the Gemma screen, target 9 of 10). Considerations come from a fixed 12-presentation list, not a complete differential. The NEWS2 triage level is a suggestion for adults (not pregnancy); the nurse confirms. Prescribing checks use a small rule set (59 drugs, 18 interactions, adult dose ceilings for 31) and miss what it lacks. Speech goes to the browser's or phone's cloud speech service. Redaction is pattern-based and imperfect. Reviewer identity is self-declared, so the two-person dispense check is procedural, not authenticated. Thai and Italian text needs native-speaker review. The public demo resets daily at 03:00 ICT.
